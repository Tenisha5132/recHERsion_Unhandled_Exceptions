Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common hormonal disorder affecting women during reproductive years and beyond. WHO notes PCOS is being increasingly recognized; NHS now uses the name polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) in places, but both names refer to the same condition. PCOS affects an estimated 10-13% of reproductive-aged women and up to 70% may be undiagnosed. It is a leading cause of irregular menstrual periods and infertility. 

Symptoms can include irregular or absent periods, heavy or painful periods for some, difficulty conceiving, excess facial or body hair, hair thinning, acne or oily skin. PCOS is associated with higher risk of gestational diabetes, type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, cardiovascular disease, obesity, sleep apnea, endometrial hyperplasia or cancer, and can impact mental health including anxiety and depression. 

Diagnosis requires at least two of: signs/symptoms of high androgens or elevated testosterone, irregular or absent periods, or polycystic ovaries on ultrasound (ovarian cysts not required). Other causes must be excluded. There is no cure, but lifestyle changes, hormonal treatments, and fertility treatments can help manage symptoms and reduce long-term risks.
